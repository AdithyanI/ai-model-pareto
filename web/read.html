How this works

Intelligence, cost and speed get published as two separate scatter plots, and each one has to drop an axis to exist. Say how long you are willing to wait and the winners change — including models that neither published chart can show, which turn out to be among the best available.

Why a model can be best and still invisible

A model is worth considering when nothing else is at least as good on every axis you care about. On a flat chart that test only runs on two axes, so a model can be dropped for being slow by a chart that never priced it, and dropped for being expensive by a chart that never timed it. The two rivals that condemned it are different models, and neither beats it overall.

Why a deadline finds them

The published smart-vs-cheap chart quietly answers a question with no clock in it: which model is the best buy if you are willing to wait forever? Say instead that you need an answer inside ten seconds, and the chart you should be reading is the same one drawn over only the models that fast — a different field, and a different frontier.

That is what the third chart does, and it is not a summary of the three-way result. It is the three-way result. Collect the leaders of every possible deadline and you get back precisely the set that survives on all three axes at once — no more, no less. Both halves follow from the same one-line argument: anything that beat a deadline’s winner outright would be no slower, so it would already be inside that deadline, beating it there. Nothing is approximated and nothing is left over.

How to read the chart

Each point is one model configuration on one host, because price and speed belong to the host rather than the model. The green line is that chart’s frontier — the best intelligence you can get at each price, or at each response time. Everything in the shaded area is beaten by something on that line, so only the diamonds are marked.

Cost and time use logarithmic axes; the field spans orders of magnitude and a linear axis would crush everything cheap into the margin. Nothing is interpolated. The frontier is drawn as a staircase rather than a curve because the space between two models contains no models — only the guarantee that a bigger budget never buys you less.

Dominance is recomputed against whatever is currently on screen, so a point is never greyed out by a rival you have filtered away. The deadlines in the split chart are taken from the spread of response times in the field being shown rather than fixed in advance, so they follow the speed measure you pick. The full method, including how the data is read and why this latency measure, is in the method note.
